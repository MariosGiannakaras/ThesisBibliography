Abstract 
 AI has revolutionized the world and begun to influence an increasing number of industries worldwide. The pressure to implement AI has been evident at both EU and national levels. AI has demonstrated significant benefits in many companies, such as improved financial performance and efficiency. However, despite this, there are still many companies, particularly manufacturing companies, that have not implemented AI. Interestingly, manufacturing companies are among those that stand to benefit the most from AI. Therefore, exploring the intentions to adopt AI in manufacturing companies becomes relevant. The concept of adopting innovation and to some extent AI adoption, is theoretically explored. However, there is a lack of empirical evidence of what hinders manufacturing companies from adopting and implementing AI. Furthermore, Swedish companies are less positive towards AI compared to global leaders. To address the identified research gap, the following research question has been formulated:   “What are the barriers and determinants that manufacturing companies in the northern 
part of Sweden face during the adoption and implementation of AI? “  The aim of this thesis is to investigate the factors influencing the adoption and implementation of AI solutions in manufacturing companies located in the northern part of Sweden, based on previous research and the empirical findings. To fulfill the purpose, we designed an exploratory qualitative study and conducted seven semi-structured interviews based on themes derived from TOE and TAM along with Rogers Innovation Diffusion.  The key findings from the study revealed that the combined TOE-TAM model along with Rogers theory and other factors like leadership and external actors can explain the intention to adopt AI technology. Further, the significant barriers to AI adoption are the lack of sufficient competence both internally and externally, another barrier was the costs of the implementation. Although we identified barriers to AI adoption, a lot of opportunities emerged as well. The determinants to adopt AI or not was the internal attitude and understanding of how the company can utilize AI. This thesis contributes with knowledge concerning the factors affecting AI adoption in manufacturing companies in the northern part of Sweden. It resulted in a revised conceptual framework that provides valuable insights for managers to consider when adopting AI. Finally, the thesis contributes with social implications that can assist in the rising concerns regarding AIs role in society.     Keywords: Artificial intelligence, AI, Sweden, Manufacturing, Industry 4.0, Technology-Organization-Environment Model, Technology Acceptance Model, Business Network, Innovation Diffusion.

1. Introduction 
 This chapter provides insight to understand the theoretical and practical background of the adoption and implementation of Artificial Intelligence particularly in the manufacturing industry and identifies the problems that occur with the adoption. Furthermore, the chapter outlines the research gap, followed by the purpose and delimitations.     
1.1 Background   “Businesses that grow by development and improvement do not die.” 
Henry Ford, February 15, 1923. 
A hot topic during these last years is the functions of Artificial Intelligence (AI) (Radhakrishnan & Chattopadhyay, 2022, p.1). AI is currently adopted in several businesses all around the world and has increased significantly between the years 2017 and 2022 (McKinsey, 2022). Although AI is a hot topic it is difficult to define what AI really is and while several articles mentioned how to adapt to AI, they don’t specify what kind of AI they should adopt. Clarification of AI or not, it is clear that AI derives business value (Mikalef & Gupta, 2021, p.1). In fact, in a report from McKinsey (2022), several businesses see a high impact on the bottom line after adopting AI, with 20 percent of EBIT coming from the use of AI. Identifying the many application areas for AI and how the world is adapting towards this rising revolutionary technology.   
Industrial revolutions have been driven by different technological advancements for a long time and throughout history different advancements have pushed paradigm shifts, we have seen these revolutions driven by mechanization, electrical energy and digitalization (Lasi et al., 2014, p.239). AI is now the new technological advancement that is one of the key drivers in the new industrial revolution called “Industry 4.0”, where manufacturing processes and business models will be revolutionized through disruptive changes (Peres et al., 2020, p.220122). The adoption of AI in manufacturing firms has a particular value, it is the industry that experiences the most cost reduction and revenue increases after adopting AI (McKinsey, 2023).   
The Swedish government has made several attempts to push the adoption of AI, particularly in the manufacturing industry (Regeringen, 2018). Moreover, the Swedish government has initiated an AI commission with the overall goal of making Sweden more competitive in the global market (Regeringen, 2023). Showing that AI has an important role not only in organizations but in society.  
The size of the AI market is growing quickly, according to Maheshwari & Jain (2024) the market size of AI is expected to be 1.8 trillion dollars in 2030 and generative AI alone is expected to grow to a market size of 1.3 trillion dollars by 2032 (Bloomberg, 2023).   
Tillväxtanalys (2021, pp.20-21) shows statistics from Sweden that one main reason why firms do not use AI is because of the costs of purchasing the technology, another barrier is also the lack of vision and strategies. Furthermore, other factors that affect the implementation are the AI understanding and skills of executives and according to Tillväxtanalys (2021, p.18), these factors are what determines if the implementation will be successful or not since executives are the ones leading the implementation. The 
 
implementation of AI is also dependent on a business networking strategy which means building valuable relationships with subcontractors and other interfirm relationships (Mazzola et al., 2009, p.387). Although there are many benefits with implementing AI, some notable individuals like Stephen Hawking and Elon Musk are addressing the potential concerns with the technology (Zhao et al., 2024, p.75). However, as Henry Ford said, businesses need to continue to develop and improve otherwise they might die.    
1.1.2 AI in businesses   Hardly does a day go by without the media bringing up the emergence of artificial intelligence (AI), AI is emerging in different robots, networks and machine learning (Zhao et al., 2024, p.75). In the past years both literature and businesses have given AI more attention, particularly in machine learning techniques. However, while AI provides tools to solve problems, there are challenges with practical implementation because of lack of knowledge when it comes to creating business value (Kitsios & Kamariotou, 2021, p.1).   
According to Mikalef & Gupta (2021, p.1) organizations need to invest in competences and resources to utilize and develop their AI investments. When understanding what is required to be developed and what must be implemented, business performance can be gained from AI. As with any new technology, organizations need to develop unique resources to effectively create business value (Mikalef & Gupta, 2021, p.2). To develop a comprehensive understanding of AI, it is good to explore the intelligence itself before implementing it to machines. Evaluating the intelligence of various technologies including AI, which requires stepping away from individual systems (Mikalef & Gupta, 2021 p.2).  
The utilization of Industrial AI towards an optimizing of processes in manufacturing has rapidly started gaining momentum. It is smarter, more efficient data-driven decision-making by gather both historical data and real-time data (Peres et al., 2020, p.220130). Moreover, are there any problems or reasons why not to use AI? For example, employment is an important factor to consider when talking about AI since it could affect individuals' well-being and some empirical studies shows that it has an impact while other doesn’t (Zhao et al., 2024, p.77).  Abrardi et al. (n.d, p.7) states that most concerns regarding employment and new technologies is that it can lead to fewer jobs. However, is this just a fear or can organizations gain from implementing AI?  
AI in management capacity has the potential to do strategic planning, create and strength relationships, and be better in decision-making and controlling (Wamba-Taguimdje et al., 2020, p.1901). It is also important to have personal skills of intelligence in AI called AI personal Expertise (AIPE) and AIPE combined with awareness could offer a more effective management of AI resources (Wamba-Taguimdje et al., 2020, p.1901). According to Wamba-Taguimdje et al. (2020, p.1901) organizations with competence in AI are more likely to make better strategic decisions with AI than those who do not have sufficient competence. 
Businesses that adopt AI continue to increase and the technology gives rising returns (McKinsey, 2019). According to McKinsey (2019) AI usage increases nearly 25 percent year-over-year, with a big jump from past years in companies that use AI across multiple areas of their organization. It is also shown that a majority of organizations that adopted AI have provided an uptick in revenues and 44 percent state that AI has reduced their costs (McKinsey, 2019).  
 
1.1.3 AI in businesses in Sweden  A survey from PWC (2024) shows that AI is about to increase the world economy with 270 000 billion SEK by 2030, and about 10 percent is going to be generated by the northern part of Europe. The survey also shows that global leaders in businesses are more positive than Swedish ones. Among global leader´s 32 percent state that they have implemented generative AI while only 16 percent of Swedish leaders have changed their technical strategy (PWC, 2024). What do leaders think about the future? Global leaders are seeing more opportunities with AI. Six out of ten global leaders argue that AI is going to increase the quality of both products and services, while only four out of ten in Sweden believes the same (PWC, 2024). Furthermore, Tillväxtanalys (2021, p.12) states that only 5 percent of Swedish companies use AI.  
There is a difference between global and Swedish business leaders which can be described because of a lack of knowledge of how AI can improve the production capacity, where Swedish leaders sees more risks than opportunities (PWC, 2024). According to the survey from PWC (2024) Sweden can be less positive to AI because of the lack of guidelines and rules on how to use it in a responsible way. Furthermore, the Swedish government presented an AI commission on December 8th, 2023 (AI Sweden, 2023). With the goal to investigate how to use AI to create opportunities, handle risks and use it in a risk-free way (AI Sweden, 2023). The commission aims to strengthen Swedish competitiveness and make Sweden a big actor in the development of AI (Regeringen, 2023).   
The survey from PWC (2024) states that the European Union aims to create better circumstances for a responsible use of AI. Moreover, the importance of clarity regarding AI is also highlighted by the EU. The absence of clearness can prevent the exploitation of the significant potential of AI which caused the EU to adopt an AI act to foster responsible AI innovations (PWC, 2024). Now it is up to companies to create frameworks and processes to develop this kind of innovation (PWC, 2024). 
Why should Sweden continue to adopt and implement AI? AI is a powerful tool that is going to help both businesses and individuals optimize and make things more effective (AI Sweden, 2023).    
1.1.4 Adoption & Implementation of AI  In the last decade, the rapid accumulation of different data formats has driven the need for new technology which has resulted in the advancements of AI techniques (Akthar et al., 2019, cited in Kitsios & Kamariotou, 2021, p.1). Technology advancement plays an important role in digital business transformation, something that disrupts industries by removing barriers between people, businesses and objects, fostering innovation and creating new products and services (Schwertner, 2017, p.388). Furthermore, Schwertner (2017, p.388) stresses that organizations must integrate digital technologies to compete and thrive in the digital era (Schwertner, 2017, p.388).   
As mentioned earlier, since the beginning of industrialization, significant technological advancements have consistently driven paradigm shifts often referred to as “industrial revolutions” (Lasi et al., 2014, p.239). The first revolution was driven by mechanization, the second by the use of electrical energy and the third by the widespread digitalization (Lasi et al., 2014, p.239). Now it is suggested that there is a fourth paradigm shift called “Industry 4.0” where AI plays a significant role in the disruptive change of structures of business models and manufacturing processes (Peres et al., 2020, p.220122).   
 
The question is how companies can leverage and adapt to the new paradigm shift. Historically organizations’ way of adapting to new technologies can be explained with the TOE framework (Chatterjee et al., 2021). The TOE framework explains that technological, organizational, and environmental characteristics are crucial characteristics when organizations want to adopt to new innovation (Hossain & Quaddus, 2011, p.239). Another model that explains the adoption of new technology is the TAM model (Davis, 1989) which explains that the perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use are what determine whether people reject or accept information technology (Davis, 1989). Chatterjee et al. (2021, p.10) further explain that bridging perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use with social, environmental, and technological factors explains the attention of adopting AI. Which indicates a TOE-TAM-based integrated model where perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use are mediating variables. Furthermore, Chatterjee et al. (2021, p.10) also discuss that competency serves as a key factor in AI adoption.   
According to McKinsey (2022) the adoption of AI has doubled since 2017. Where the adoption of AI in Supply Chain Management among other areas are driven by cost reductions, enhanced efficiency, and better decision-making (Hangl et al., 2023, p.1). Although, Hangl et al. (2023, p.1) also identify some barriers including an unwillingness to change, management issues and a lack of confidence and understanding of AI, something that they can overcome by engaging all stakeholders.     
The adoption of AI in the global arena is constantly rising. However, in Sweden, the adoption of AI in organizations has been slower, something that may be because of AI skepticism and the lack of regulations and safety systems (PWC, 2024). Although according to Tillväxtanalys (2021, p.21) legal and ethical issues are the least significant barrier among the major barriers to AI adoption. Instead, Tillväxtanalys (2021, p.21) states that the more significant barriers are the cost of adopting AI, the lack of an AI strategy and the lack of competency.       
1.1.5 Manufacturing companies in Sweden Manufacturing is defined as businesses involved in altering materials, substances, or components through mechanical, physical, or chemical processes to create new products, it can also be the assembling of several parts of manufactured goods (Levinson, 2017, p.2). According to Tillväxtverket (n.d) there were 54 000 manufacturing companies in Sweden in 2021, representing the largest share of employment with 18 percent of the total employment in Sweden. The manufacturing industry is also one of the leading sectors in terms of net turnover and value-added (Tillväxtverket, n.d). However, to continue creating value, the manufacturing industry will need to constantly develop.   
Since the 20th century, the Swedish manufacturing industry has undergone a transformative shift where the Swedish government has initiated a new strategy towards new industrialization called “Smart industry” (Sundberg et al., 2019, p.731). In a report by the Swedish government, the goal of the new strategy is that Swedish industrial companies will lead in digital development and utilize digitalization opportunities. Implementation focuses on stimulating key digital technologies, broad application across industries, fostering innovative business models, addressing knowledge needs, and adapting regulatory conditions for the digital era (Regeringen, 2018, p.14). One of the other main objectives of the initiative is to make Swedish manufacturing companies more competitive (Regeringen, 2018, p.6).   
 
With a new focus toward a digital era, driven by several factors such as the government, society and technology it is clear that Swedish manufacturing companies need to develop. According to Sundberg et al. (2019, p.732) there is evidence that manufacturing companies have adjusted to the development by increasing investments in information technology, from 7 billion SEK to 12 billion SEK between the years 2016 and 2017.    
1.1.6 Business Network A business network is an arena where different actors can work together uniquely and it provides wide diversity when these actors interact (Ford & Mouzas, 2013, p.440). To meet the competitive drivers resulting from technological advancements and globalization companies must consider a business networking strategy which means building interfirm relationships with subcontractors (Mazzola et al., 2009, p.387). There are different objectives in how organizations have organized their networks, and three main objectives are to achieve efficiency, gather knowledge and engage in global expansion (Mazzola et al., 2009, p.387).   
Applying Industry 4.0 frequently requires integration with lean manufacturing principles. However, numerous companies, especially small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), encounter challenges in implementing Industry 4.0 due to a lack of clarity in combining it with Lean practices (Vargas et al., 2024, p.95). To ensure lean manufacturing, companies are heavily dependent on subcontractors and other interfirm relationships indicating the importance of business networks. With lean manufacturing in place, the integration of Industry 4.0 becomes easier and enables better operational performance (Kumar et al., 2024, p.246).     
1.2 Problem Background   AI is the topic of the century and companies must find ways to adapt to the new technology and many sectors have already started the implementation. The importance of new technology adoption is emphasized by the Swedish government who want to advance the Swedish manufacturing industry by implementing a new strategy called “Smart industry” (Regeringen, 2018). The overall goal is to make Swedish companies in the manufacturing industry leaders in digital development and more competitive on the global arena (Regeringen, 2018). However, the manufacturing industry as a whole is falling behind. According to a survey by McKinsey (2023) the manufacturing industry is the industry that uses generative AI the least. Although the survey also states that manufacturing companies are the ones that benefit the most from AI with cost reductions and increasing revenues (McKinsey, 2023).  
PWCs survey (2024) states that Swedish leaders are less positive to implement and adopt AI than global leaders. Indicating why Swedish businesses can be behind in the implementation of AI in their companies. In a report from Tillväxtanalys (2021, p.12) only 5 percent of Swedish companies use AI compared to 14 percent of Japanese companies. To prevent slow development, the Swedish government applied an “AI Commission” in December 2023 to not only push Sweden to become a leader in digital development and AI but also to make Sweden an important actor for other countries (Regeringen, 2023, p.1).   
The Swedish government started the initiative already in 2018 but with a focus on the manufacturing industry (Regeringen, 2018). The purpose of the initiative was to push the 
 
implementation of Industry 4.0 and make Sweden a leader in digital development and create more competitive manufacturing companies (Regeringen, 2018, pp.6-14).   
The adoption of AI shows a significant impact on the bottom line, with 20 percent of EBIT being derived from the implementation (McKinsey, 2022). Despite this, the implementation is not as high as expected. Some barriers that companies face in the adoption process are the absence of sufficient competence, the expenses related to adopting AI as well as the lack of a strategy for AI (Tillväxtanalys, 2021, p.21). Further, manufacturing companies might be less accepting of errors and demand more accuracy and efficiency which can also be a barrier to the adoption (Tillväxtanalys, 2021, p.21).   
The role of networks in AI and Industry 4.0 adoption process is also significant and is especially explained in lean manufacturing (Vargas et al., 2024, p.95). Where the network between the organization, subcontractors and other interfirm relationships plays an important role in enabling lean practices (Vargas et al., 2024, p.95). When these are in place adopting AI and Industry 4.0 becomes much easier.   In conclusion, there is an obvious demand from the government to make Sweden more prominent and push Swedish manufacturing companies to implement and be compatible with the “Smart industry” which includes Industry 4.0 (Regeringen, 2018). Although, Sweden is falling behind in the implementation (Tillväxtanalys, 2021, p.12). It is therefore interesting to examine what barriers and determinants there could be in the adoption process.    
1.3 Research GAP  We are in a very digital age and businesses need to reduce periods of waiting and have more consciousness in the technological environment, something that needs to be done faster than in previous decades (Kitsios & Kamariotou, 2021, p.1). Making several organizations adopt emerging technologies that have high performance and advantages of being competitive (Kitsios & Kamariotou, 2021, p.1). Among the different technologies, AI has a significant position. Furthermore, there is little research regarding Swedish manufacturing firms and how they handle the adoption of AI and industry 4.0.  
There is current literature that highlights the key of AI adoption and use of AI in organizational settings (Enholm et al., 2022, p.1709). However, Enholm et al. (2022, p.1709) state that there are certain gaps, where there is still lack of a holistic understanding of how AI is adopted and how it is used in organizations, also what the main value-generating mechanisms are.   
Chatterjee et al. (2021) study does not consider the vulnerable issues of security that might obstruct the progress of the adoption of AI in organizations. While looking into the literature, it can be considered that the literature covered much about how AI can be connected to business value and how to adopt AI in the wide organization. However, Chattarjee et al. (2021, p.2) also state that further research on how organizations can prepare to leverage the significant potential of AI is needed.   
What can be seen from the review of the literature is how Swedish organizations adopt and implement AI into their businesses and according to PWC (2024), Swedish companies are behind in implementing the new technology compared to global organizations. As stated before, according to the survey from McKinsey (2023), it is manufacturing companies that are behind in the adoption of AI although they are the ones 
 
that gain the most from the implementation. Highlighting that there is a research gap in what barriers and determinants organizations face when considering the adoption of AI, especially in the manufacturing industry.   
According to Chatterjee et al. (2021, p.11), future research should also utilize the combined TAM-TOE model for further understanding of the implementation and adoption of AI in manufacturing companies.   
The connection between business networks and manufacturing companies can be viewed in a lot of literature. Although Vargas et al. (2024, p.115) argue how networks influences the adoption of AI it is not extensively explained and also fails to explain the role of subcontractors and other interfirm relationships. Moreover Tillväxttanalys (2021, pp.19-20) assumes that interfirm relationships and coordination across organizations work as a driver for AI adoption but there is little evidence of this in literature. Indicating there is a need for further research on the role of business networks when manufacturing companies adopt AI.   
In an article by Tillväxtanalys (2021, p.21), the authors assume that manufacturing companies experience cost as a barrier of adopting AI, they also believe that manufacturing companies are less tolerant of errors and require a higher level of precision than for example marketing companies, however, it is not empirically explored. Furthermore, Tillväxtanalys (2021, p.24) argues that there is a need for a conception of the different drivers of AI adoption as well as the different barriers.     
1.4 Research question  The identified research outlines the importance of technology development and advancement. While also describing the lack of understanding of how AI can be adopted and used in organizations and how they can leverage the potential of AI. Furthermore, the Swedish government is pushing the adoption of the new technology through different incentives to make Sweden more competitive, especially in the manufacturing industry (Regeringen, 2018, p.6). Although, according to PWC (2024) Swedish companies are behind in the implementation of AI. Especially the manufacturing industry is behind in the adoption, even though it is stated that they will benefit the most from AI (McKinsey, 2023). It is clear that there is a need for future research regarding what hinders the adoption of AI in Swedish manufacturing companies. To address the identified research gap, the following research question have been formulated with a geographical limitation to manufacturing companies based in the northern part of Sweden to give a more precise assessment and representation of the results.   
“What are the barriers and determinants that manufacturing companies in the northern part of Sweden face during the adoption and implementation of AI? “ 
 
1.5 Purpose    The purpose of this thesis is to provide an understanding of what affects the implementation and adoption of AI solutions in manufacturing companies in the northern part of Sweden, based on previous research and the empirical findings. Moreover, the thesis aims to explain the different barriers and determinants that occur in the process of adopting the technology. Furthermore, the intention is that the study will result in a framework that can explain the different factors that influence the implementation and adoption in particularly manufacturing companies. Which is accomplished through a 
 
qualitative study where interviews with manufacturing companies based in the northern part of Sweden as well as external consultants who have competence in manufacturing and AI technology are conducted.     Table 1. Summary of research question and purpose

1.6 Delimitations To provide an understanding of the barriers and determinants of AI adoption in manufacturing companies, a geographical delimitation was necessary. The researchers understand that the thesis won’t be able to present a full view of the adoption of AI in manufacturing companies in general, therefore delimiting the study to the northern part of Sweden. Moreover, due to the timeframe of this thesis, the chosen companies are relatively big and not micro. Enabling better comparison between the companies and better representation of companies in the northern part of Sweden at the same level since larger organizations can both be ahead but at the same time have slower processes.    Furthermore, companies chosen for this thesis are companies in the manufacturing industry, due to manufacturing companies being behind in the implementation of AI. Additionally, another delimitation is to interview the people who possess the right and broad view of the company. The right people in this thesis are defined as individuals who are in a position where they have a broad view and the required knowledge to answer questions about both internal and external factors that could impact the adoption process. Although a delimitation does not include insurance that the respondents have sufficient knowledge about AI, even though that would be preferable. 2.3 Innovation Adoption Innovation can be seen as an idea, a product, a program, or technology that is adopt by a company or unit and that is new for them (Hameed et al., 2012, p.358). Furthermore, Hamed et al. (2012, p.359) states that adoption of an innovation is a process that could result in implementation. Innovation is studied to be one of the overall key factors in success for businesses, and adoption relate to a decision of how to use an innovation in the business (Frambach, & Schillewaert, 2002, p.163). Since both innovation and adoption is a process in organizations its need to be clarified how the process generally works.   First, in an innovation process, there are often three stages, they are initiation, adoption, and implementation (Frambach, & Schillewaert, 2002, p.164). The first step, initiation is where an organization becomes aware of the innovation, and from the awareness the organizations form attitudes and evaluates the innovation (Frambach, & Schillewaert, 2002, p.164). Moreover, the authors explain the implementation stage as the stage where organizations decide to use the innovation and purchase it. Additionally, the adoption process is the very beginning of the implementation stage, here the acceptance becomes important and how to integrate it within the firm is also a significant phase in adoption (Frambach, & Schillewaert, 2002, p.164). Similarly, Damanpour, & Schneider (2006, p.216) define Organizational innovation as the adoption of a new product, service, or technology, and the adoption can be a result of managerial choice or enforced by external requirements. Damanpour, & Schneider (2006, p.216) describe the process of innovation and adoption as a process, where adoption is the second phase of the process.   There are some different characteristics when it comes to adoption and adopters. First, the environmental characteristics describe the external requirements, it can also describe cultural and geographical conditions, where the authors could see that innovation and adoption is more common in growing communities (Damanpour, & Schneider, 2006, p.225). There are also managerial factors that can affect how organizations adopt innovations. Damanpour, & Schneiders (2006, p.228) study identified that variables like age, gender, and education did not have a significant effect on either adoption or implementation. The biggest finding from the managerial characteristics is that top managers´ attitudes to entrepreneurship and competition could have a positive persuasion to the process of innovation (with initiation, adoption, and implementation) (Damanpour, & Schneider, 2006, p.229).   Frambach, & Schillewaert (2002, p.165) highlights the importance of different characteristics and how they influence the process. They also highlighted that size could affect innovation adoption. It can be argued that larger organizations are in need of adopting innovation to support and improve the organizational performance, however, smaller organizations are more innovative because they are more flexible (Frambach, & Schillewaert, 2002, p.165).   Innovation adoption seems to be good if firms want to evolve. One big innovation adoption is the IT (information technology) innovation adoption. What does IT innovation adoption mean in an organizational context? IT innovation adoption in organizations can be explained like a response to a change in environmental settings, where it is required for continuing with their organizational operations (Hameed et al., 2012, p.359). Organizational level of adoption begins when the organization seeks 
 
learning of the innovation until the acquisition of the technology. The IT innovation has the same process as innovation adoption, as mentioned earlier. Hameed et al. (2012, p.374) explain a model for IT adoption, with a combination of other models like the technology adoption model (TAM) integrated, to see how well the adoption process could lead to IT implementation.   
2.3.1 Rogers Innovation Diffusion Theory Rate of adoption is when innovation is adopted by a member of a social organization, there are some attributes that can explain the rate of adoption of an innovation – they are; relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability (Rogers, 2002, p.221).   Relative advantage is the measure of the innovation being better than the idea it replaces (Rogers, 2002, p.229). The attribute is often expressed as economic profitability and social prestige and it is important to the adopters and to see which advantages that is most important (Rogers, 2002, p.229). The main reason to adopt an innovation through relative advantage according to Rogers (2002, p.233) is if the innovation is better than the existing one, including low initial cost, a saving of time and effort, and immediacy reward. These measurements can explain why adoption can be very slow and take some time, which can be a barrier or an obstacle (Rogers, 2002, pp.233-234).   The second attribute compatibility is the level to which an innovation is coherent with existing values, past experiences, and needs of potential adopters. Where an idea that is closer to values and past experiences is more compatible with an organization (Rogers, 2002, p.240). An innovation can be more or less compatible with cultural values, earlier pioneered idea, or if the clients need an innovation (Rogers, 2002, p.240). A barrier to adoption of innovation can be incompatibility with the organization’s values and beliefs. It can be easier to adopt an innovation if it is compatible with a previous idea, another measurement is how compatible the new idea is with the requested needs (Rogers, 2002, pp.240-246). Even though meeting the needs is important, the adopters may not be aware of the needs they have before the new idea or its consequences occurs (Rogers, 2002, p.246).   Complexity is the third attribute that explains how difficult an innovation is to understand and use. A new idea can be classified as complex or simple, some innovations are clearer in their meaning to adopters (Rogers, 2002, p.257). Furthermore, Rogers (2002, p.257) state that complexity is not as important than relative advantage or compatibility for many innovations and adopters, but for some new innovations complexity can be a significant obstacle to adoption.   Attribute number four is trialability which means how an innovation may be tested on a controlled basis. New innovations that can be tested are generally more likely to be adopted than innovations that are not (Rogers, 2002, p.258). An innovation that is more tested and designed to be easier to try will, as Rogers (2002, p.258) states, more likely have an instant rate of adoption. Trialability can be more important to early adopter than late adopters because it is easier to the ones that are late adopters to surround themselves with ones that already adopted the innovation (Rogers, 2002, p.258).   
 
The last attribute to rate of adoption is observability where the results of an innovation are observable to others (Rogers, 2002, p.258). Which is positive related to rate of adoption.   The diffusion of innovation theory is complex, and it is primally focus is to collect information about adopting to innovations and cutting uncertainty (Uzumcu & Acilmis 2023). According to the authors Uzumcu & Acilmis (2023) early adopters are role models for innovation, on the other hand late adopters are more suspicious about innovations and wait with the adoption until it becomes more known.

2.3.3 Technology, Organization & Environment (TOE) Framework The TOE (Technology-Organization-Environment) framework explains the relationship between three different factors to understand the adoption of technological innovations (Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990, cited in, Zhu et al., 2006, pp.1559-1560). Technology describes the existing use of technology and the new relevant technologies to the firm, the organization involves measures such as scope, size, and managerial structure while the environment explains the broader business arena with the industry, competitors, and government interactions (Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990, cited in, Zhu et al., 2006, pp.1559-1560). The TOE framework has a lot of similarities with the diffusion of innovation theory by Rogers since the theory also highlights technological factors 
 
alongside internal and external organizational factors as drivers for technology adoption and diffusion (Zhu et al., 2006, p.1560).

2.3.4 Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) The technology acceptance model (TAM) developed by Fred Davis, is a model that intends to explain users’ acceptance processes for different IT innovations (Hong et al., 
 
2006, p.1822). It is a commonly used model that defines the users’ adoption of innovation by understanding the perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness (Hong et al., 2006, p.1822). Perceived usefulness is the individual confidence that a system or technology can increase or improve performance and be beneficial to the organization by for example enabling efficiency and higher returns (Davis, 1989, p.320). Perceived ease of use, on the other hand, is the individual confidence that the system or technology can be implemented with little effort. The perception is that if the system or technology is easier to use it is more likely to be accepted and adopted (Davis, 1989, p.320).

 The reason the TAM model is widely used and significantly popular is because it understands the behavioral factors that affect technology adoption (Bryan & Zuva, 2021, p.139). Moreover, it explains that it doesn’t matter how good the technology is if the user doesn’t perceive the technology as useful and easy to adopt (Bryan & Zuva, 2021, p.139). Although TAM has a significant focus on the perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use it doesn’t consider critical social aspects of information systems development (Malatji et al., 2020, p.115). Furthermore, other factors like cost and organizational constraints that might impact user adoption are not represented (Malatji et al., 2020, p.115). Additionally, the TAM model also has some limitations like measuring behavior since it involves factors like personality, individual traits and societal values (Malatji et al., 2020, p.115).  Although TAM is a good model for understanding the adoption of technology, researchers have proposed an integrated model for technology adoption where the TAM and TOE models can improve the explanation of technology adoption (Chattarjee et al., 2021). Combining the two models, perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use together with social, environmental, and technological factors they can explain the intention to adopting AI, especially in an Industry 4.0 context (Chattarjee et al., 2021).

2.4 Manufacturing Industry   Manufacturing goes back to the very beginning of mankind, 2.6 million years ago when the very first manufacturing technology “cutting” was invented (Roser, 2016). Manufacturing has since then developed and has been “the driver of economic growth, structural change and catch-up” (Naudé & Szirmai, 2012, cited in, Herman 2016, p.976). It is a driver that according to the EU Commission creates a spill-over effect which means that the demand in the manufacturing industry leads to half as much increase in demand in other sectors (Herman, 2016, p.977). In 2012, the manufacturing industry represented 16 percent of the global GDP and 14 percent of the global employment (McKinsey, 2012).   As mentioned before, manufacturing is defined as businesses involved in altering materials, substances, or components through mechanical, physical, or chemical processes to create new products, it can also be the assembling of several parts of manufactured goods (Levinson, 2017, p.2). The manufacturing companies that manage to do it most efficiently while enabling the best quality are the ones that can be most competitive (Wacker, 1996, p.484). The author further stresses the importance of lead time as a measure of performance and Japanese companies that had a focus on lead time showed great success (Wacker, 1996, p.485). Due to this, the concept of lean manufacturing was introduced, and Japan was the first country to apply lean practices (Gupta & Jain, 2013, p.241). The lean manufacturing concept means eliminating any waste from the organization such as inventory, since the waste is something that does not add value to the product (Gupta & Jain, 2013, p.241). The lean manufacturing concept changes the view of products from the value coming from an internal point of view to changing to a customer point of view and the overall goal of lean manufacturing is to produce products as fast as possible with the best quality for the lowest cost (Gupta & Jain, 2013, p.241). The process is also referred to as Just-in-time (JIT) (Gupta & Jain, 2013, p.241).   With lean manufacturing and JIT, the goal becomes to decrease the time from raw material to finished goods and the impact of a small disruption in the supply chain can lead to a significant increase in lead time (Chang & Lin, 2019, p.298).  For example, a 10-minute fire at Philips plant caused a one-year effect for their customers, the company Ericsson said that the disruption caused them a 2.34 billion dollar loss (Chang & Lin, 2019, p.298). The actions to improve or assist lean manufacturing processes can therefore play a significant role.   The integration of AI in lean manufacturing can potentially improve efficiency and productivity by enabling better connections between equipment, information and people (Sharma et al., 2023, p.390).  Additionally, Sharma et al. (2023, p.390) explain that AI can improve productivity by reducing human involvement in tasks where AI and machinery outperform human capabilities. AI can generally generate better product quality, cost optimization and reduced lead times. The implementation of AI in combination with lean practices can create cultural changes with a more flexible workflow and an organization that constantly changes (Sharma et al., 2023, p.390). Moreover, the application of AI can help to identify problems in the production lines, analyze different customer complaints and get insight into how to improve processes (Sharma et al., 2023, p.390). Manufacturing companies have strategically incorporated AI technologies and leveraged their capabilities by revolutionizing production processes through the development of smarter and more flexible and eco-friendly systems that align with Industry 4.0 (Chattarjee et al., 2021, pp.120879-120880). Ultimately, what matters 
 
in the implementation is a comprehensive understanding of the existing processes and an organization that embraces constant improvements (Sharma et al., 2023, p.390).

Nevertheless, only 4 percent of the Swedish manufacturing industry has adopted AI technologies compared to the ICT (Information, communication & technology) sector where 21 percent has adopted AI technology (Tillväxtanalys, 2020, p.14).    Sweden is considered a high-cost country with especially high labor costs. The adoption of AI and the implementation of more automated processes can therefore decrease the need for human capacity and avoid high labor costs, however, it causes a paradox (Edh Mirzaei et al., 2021, p.281). 2.4.2 Industry 4.0 Industry 4.0 can be explained like the fourth industrial revolution (Ghobakhloo 2018, p.910). Industry 4.0 is the industrial revolution that is existing now. AI is a new technological innovation that is one of the keys to the new revolution, where manufacturing processes and business models will go through huge changes (Peres et al., 2020, p.220122). It is the technology that drives Industry 4.0 and its innovation and changes in the businesses environment, both in

According to Ahmed et al. 3. Scientific Methodology  
 Following chapter will provide a research philosophy and understanding about ontological, epistemological, and axiological considerations. Thereafter a research approach will inform what type of approach (deductive, inductive, or abductive) this thesis will possess.   
3.1 Research Philosophy  People have different philosophies and assumptions about the world and knowledge nature, research paradigm is like a framework that could guide the researchers how a study should be conducted (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.39). To sort these different individual thoughts and to understand which philosophy that is most suitable, it is good to understand the two main research paradigms. These two different paradigms are positivism and interpretivism. Positivism comes from the natural science and its assumptions are that social reality is one single thing and objective, and the reality is not affected on the investing the researcher does (Saunders et al., 2016, p.135). The other main paradigm, interpretivism occurred in the criticism of positivism, the paradigm has the assumption that reality is in our minds and that it is subjective and various, and therefore is it influenced by the investigation of it (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.40). The interpretivist paradigm highlights that humans are different from physical phenomena because humans create different meanings (Saunders et al., 2016, p.140).  These two main paradigms both include assumptions about social reality (ontological assumption), the role of values that could be influenced by the researcher (axiological assumption), and human knowledge (epistemological assumption) (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.43). Interpretivism is the paradigm that this study should have, because of the subjective assumptions. Enabling this paper to boost our method and our data collection, along with the research strategy. In the following sections below, it will be discussed how the chosen philosophy are connected to the three components of the interpretivist paradigm.   
3.1.1 Ontology Ontology is a philosophical assumption about the nature of reality, and it is a assumptions about what it means for something to exist (Bell & Bryman, 2019, p.26). The most significant question ontology focus of is whether the social phenomena should be comprehended as external observers or if it is made real by activities of people and meanings which the observers connect to them (Bell & Bryman, 2019, p.26). If the phenomena are understood as external observe it is referred as objectivism, and if it is referred activities from humans and meanings from those who observe, it is called subjectivism or constructionism.  To understand ontology, it is helpful to understand what objectivity and subjectivity means. Bell & Bryman (2019, p.26) views the objectivism as an ontological position that implies that the reality is about external factors beyond the research that is perceived, and the researchers are not able to influence it, you can say that you have an objective reality that is independent from the observer. Objectivism is based on that there is only one reality, assuming everyone has the same view of reality (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.43). Furthermore, this assumption could have a visible truth and is completely disconnected 
 
from the researcher. Subjectivism, or constructivism challenges the objective approach, the assumption argues that reality is shaped by the actions and understandings of people and not just one external reality (Bell & Bryman, 2019, p.27). Within subjectivism each person has their own sense of reality, and therefore the reality is multiple (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.43).   Due to the purpose of studying organizations in their surroundings, and how they do the process when it comes to adoption and implementation and therefore, it becomes natural to approve a subjective perspective contained by the ontological assumption for this thesis. Since this research is designed on interactions with different people in different positions in organizations, the objectivism and it´s perspectives are not suited. Moreover, this study is affected by the different views from the organizations and their processes, therefore, objectivism is not the perspective that this study aligns with.     
3.1.2 Epistemology Epistemology is the philosophical assumption of knowledge and how we can gain knowledge from the reality, it agrees to answer the question of how research would be conducted (Bell & Bryman, 2019, p.29). The epistemological approach can be different and vary if the research is seen from a positivism-paradigm or an interpretivism-paradigm. While ontology relevance seems rather abstract, in epistemology is it more obvious (Saunders et al., 2016, p.127).  Positivism holds the epistemology in an objective position and its information is existing externally and the appropriate way to use it is to gather data and measures them with survey and other instruments that is extern and not influenced by the researcher (Bell & Bryman, 2019, p.30). Furthermore Collis & Hussey (2021, p.43) describes epistemology in the positivism approach as observable and that the study should maintain an independent and objective stance. On the other hand, interpretivism sees epistemology as a study of the world and has formed from the critical standpoints of positivism. The philosophy believes in the explanation of human behavior and understanding things like, “how” and “why” in a social action, containing the processes that align with it rather than just one way to look at the action (Bell & Bryman, 2019, p.31). The approach attempts to reduce the distance between the researcher and the study that has been researched and make them involved (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.43). It is valuable to understand different consequences of epistemological assumptions in relation to the chosen method (Saunders et al., 2016, p.127).   This thesis will look at reality through a subjective view, therefore it is natural to have an interpretive philosophy on epistemology. All businesses are unique and has its own solutions of their complex situations, and the individuals that are in an organization has different viewpoints. With interpretivism is it easier to understand these situations and collect the data that is needed and to interact with the respondent with interviews make it natural to have an interpretivist standpoint.   
3.1.3 Axiology Axiology is the philosophical assumption of values, which means the way values will influence the research process, and notably how the values of the researcher influence the research (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.43). However, it is not only the researchers’ values but also the values of the research participants (Saunders et al., 2016, p.128). Axiology 
 
can be viewed from both a positivism-paradigm as well as an interpretivism-paradigm and it is important to disclose the paradigm beforehand.   The objective view of axiology which is in line with the positivism-paradigm believes that the research process is free of own values and are unbiased (Saunders et al., 2016, p.129). Subsequently, which means that the researcher is completely independent of what they are studying and views the things they are studying as objects (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.43). As positivists are value-free and independent from what they are studying, the researchers believe that the object that they are studying are not affected by their study and believe that the objects were there before the study and will remain unaffected after their study (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.43). Interpretivists on the other hand, who have a subjective view do not consider the researcher value-free and that the findings are unbiased, the interpretivists also actively engage in the subject that they are studying (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.43). The choice of data collection can for example show the choice of research philosophy, if the researcher chooses a qualitative approach with interviews, it suggests that the researcher values personal interaction with the participants instead of collecting their responses through an anonymous questionnaire (Saunders et al., 2016, p.128).   The thesis will continue in line with the interpretivism approach, and it is therefore logical to do so in axiology as well. The topic of the research is influenced by the interests of the authors and thus the research can’t be considered value-free. It is therefore of sincere importance that the authors can make sure that the study will not be biased, nor affected by the authors’ interests.   
3.2 Research Approach  When achieving research, you need to be able to clarify different theories and methods, as Bell & Bryman (2019, p.19) state, to gain knowledge about the study researchers needs to collect and analyze data to form theoretical understandings of the world. Studies are built on three approaches to develop a method, they are deductive-, inductive-, and abductive approach (Saunders et al., 2016, p.144). When choosing an approach, the researcher’s perspective depends on which philosophy that is used, the topics nature, and which resources is available, lastly what timeframe they researcher has. All these three approaches have different philosophies, and it can come natural which approach that is aimed for this thesis.   A deductive approach is an analysis with a conceptual and theoretical formation which is developed with empirical observations, this method can be described as moving from the general to a practical view, including collecting data of variables that is identified and central (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.8). The deductive approach is dominant in natural sciences and are often controlled (Saunders et al., 2016, p.146). A deductive approach is often built on a hypothesis that is linked with positivism, and the researcher must deduce the hypothesis and translate it to more operational terms meaning that it is important to specify how data can be collected and relate that to the hypothesis (Bell & Bryman, 2019, p.20). Saunders et al. (2016, p.146) describe the deductive approach often aligns with quantitative methods, which is based on hypothesis and collecting data from surveys and other instruments, and therefore it is important to select the data from a quantitative perspective.   
 
Since this thesis is not about proving validity of already existing theory or finding out the reality through variables and hypothesizes to find out what is rejected or not. This study aims to investigate different consequences, barriers, and outcomes from organizations adoption processes when adopting AI, and how it could impact a company´s performance. This research is strongly dependent on the respondents and can vary from organizations to organizations, and being expressed more in details rather than in statistics and values that aligns with deduction, and therefore it seems logical to not focus on the deduction approach.   However, inductive approach is an alternative approach to the deductive one. The inductive approach is developed from interpretation of empirical reality, it evolves from individual observations to trying to find general patterns (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.8). It is often formed by interviews of a sample, the purpose with inductive approach is to understand the problem better, the researcher’s main purpose is to make interviews easier to analyze and form a theory (Saunders et al., 2016, p.147). Inductive approach is not as strict as deductive approach, it is softer and tries to explain the problems through interviews and describing emotions and other “softer” instruments and can have a more flexible structure. Inductive approach is often linked to qualitative research, a study with smaller samples of subjects might be better for the inductive approach than larger surveys and therefore is linked to qualitative data that can establish different views of the phenomena and not seeing one single reality (Saunders et al., 2016, p.147).   Even though inductive research is not based on hypotheses, it can be based on theories. This thesis is linked to different theories, but the aim of the study is to investigate the processes in companies, since that is the purpose, it could be said that inductive approach is more linked to this thesis. Moreover, researchers have more opportunities to create patterns from interviews and since the thesis is built on drawing patterns and different lines through different viewpoints it is easier to analyze data with this framework.   The third approach is the abductive approach and can be described like moving back and forth between deduction and induction, abductive approach could match with different business research (Saunders et al., 2016, p.148). Abduction can, like Bell & Bryman (2019, p.24) explains, be suggested as a way of overcoming limitations that is linked to deduction and induction, it can be said that is starts with a surprise or struggle and then seeks to describe it. The observation of the surprise could be worked out with plausible theories, these surprises can occur during the writing process due to Saunders et al. (2016, p.148). Abduction engages the researcher to select the best explanation from the data or other competing explanation, abductive approach highlights the researcher to think rationally and cognitive when forming a theory (Bell & Bryman, 2019, p.24).   Using an abductive approach sounds good since it is a mix of deduction and induction, however, it can be hard to understand it in practice and it can be challenging to mix these two approaches when using interpretivism as philosophy and when qualitative data is aimed to be collected. Therefore, the abductive approach may not be the right choice for this thesis for logical explanations, even though abduction has its advantages.   Regarding this thesis, it has an evident connection with interpretivism since it is qualitative and based on a subjective vision on the reality. The process with adopting new innovation can be seen and worked with in different ways, and therefore it is not suited with one single reality since there is no general way to do that. The approach of this thesis 
 
is more aligned with induction. Furthermore, this thesis aims to have a small sample of observations and subjects and might be better to also have larger amount of data to draw a general conclusion and therefore have a more deductive approach. Again, the intention with this study is to work with smaller samples and qualitative data to fulfill the needs of this research to underline the consequences, barriers, and outcomes from organizations adoption processes when adopting AI. Since this study intends to in somehow look outside the “natural” way to do a process it is also maintained with interpretivism and an inductive approach.

4.1 Research Design  A crucial part when designing the research is to describe the reason for the study being conducted and the purpose of the study (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.5). A study can be described as either exploratory, descriptive, explanatory, or predictive research (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.5). How you choose to formulate the research question will determine which of these aspects you will engage in (Saunders et al., 2016, p.174). The exploratory research aims to provide a more general understanding of a phenomenon when there is little or no available previous research (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.5). It often means gathering insights about a topic through open questions, therefore a common way of conducting exploratory research is through interviews with experts, focus group interviews and conducting in-depth individual interviews (Saunders et al., 2016, p.175). However, Collis & Hussey (2021, p.5) states that it could also include quantitative studies like observations and historical analysis. The characteristics of exploratory research can be viewed in the research question since exploratory research focuses on “How” and “What” and similarly the questions that you ask in the data collection will begin the same (Saunders et al., 2016, p.5). The exploratory research often includes a large amount of data and impressions which means that it is not uncommon that the researcher can’t provide a conclusive response to the problem but rather push for further research (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.5).   For this thesis it is reasonable to employ exploratory approach as the research aims to get an insight into a topic through interviews and the research question also includes a “what”. Although the study aims to get as close to a conclusive response as possible it is a comprehensive study that might not result in a complete answer to the research question but might lead to implications for further research.          Moving on to the descriptive research where the aim is to describe and identify the different phenomena and therefore extend the exploratory research by presenting evidence derived from empirical findings (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.5). Therefore, in descriptive research, the research question instead often begins with “Who”, “When”, “Where”, “How” and “What” (Saunders et al., 2016, p.175). This research, however, is not a descriptive study since the aim is not to present evidence from empirical findings.   The next purpose of studies is explanatory research which is instead a continuation of the descriptive research and extends the descriptive research by providing an explanation of the cause and effect of the studied phenomenon (Collis & Hussey, 2016, p.5). Like descriptive research, it is likely that the explanatory research includes or begins with “Why” and “How” (Saunders et al., 2016, p.176). However explanatory research intends to study a problem or a situation and understand the phenomenon by testing hypotheses and the variables’ causal relationship (Collis & Hussey, 2016, p.5). Since this research won’t be tested through hypotheses and casual relationships between variables, explanatory research won’t be the approach of this study. Lastly, predictive research goes 
 
even beyond the latter perspective, it intends to make predictions of future events based on general conclusions drawn from an analysis of phenomena (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.6). Moreover, the answers to future and current events might be answered through “Where”, “Why” and “How” (Collis & Hussey, 2016, p.6). The aim of this study is not to be predictive about current and future events and therefore not the purpose of the study.   In conclusion, the purpose of this study is to conduct exploratory research by providing a general understanding of the topic and through a subjective view gathering information. Moreover, the goal is to extend the knowledge, fill the research gap and answer the research question.     Moving on to the data collection method. There are mainly two different methodological choices, the research can either be quantitative or qualitative but there can also be a mix of the methods (Saunders et al., 2016, p.165). The quantitative method is distinguished from the qualitative method in its way of collecting data, the data in quantitative research is numerical and will be analyzed using statistical methods (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.6). The qualitative method on the other hand is the collection of qualitative data and using analytical methods that are non-numerical (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.7).   Quantitative methods are often linked to the collection of data through for example surveys or questionnaires where the analysis is focused on the numerical part with graphs and statistics (Saunders et al., 2016, p.165). Furthermore, quantitative research is often referred to as being deductive with a positivist approach where the social reality is objective (Bell & Bryman, 2019, p.164). Qualitative methods are instead linked to the collection of data through for example interviews, where the analysis is focused on non-numerical data or by categorizing data (Saunders et al., 2016, p.165). Qualitative research is often linked to being inductive and having an interpretive approach where the studied phenomenon is viewed subjectively (Saunders et al., 2016, p.168).    The mix method on the other hand, is a method that combines the techniques for data collection from both quantitative and qualitative.  The mixed method recognizes that there is an external objective reality but emphasizes that individual interpretations are influenced by social conditioning, combining realist ontology with interpretivist epistemology (Saunders et al., 2016, p.169). Therefore, the research may begin with quantitative analysis and then for further understanding conduct qualitative methods to explore perceptions shaped by social contexts (Saunders et al., 2016, p.169).   For this thesis, the methodology will be qualitative, due to the aim of this study which is to explain the barriers and determinants perceived by the respondents in the adoption and implementation of AI. Throughout the thesis, the approach will be inductive and hold a subjective view which is in line with the interpretivism paradigm, calling for the use of a qualitative method. Moreover, the data collection method will be interviews which furthermore aligns with the qualitative method. This research aims to dig deep into the topic and therefore values the depth that can be drawn from smaller samples rather than surveys with larger sample sizes but less informative.       
4.2 Data Collection  The following section will further discuss the choice of how to conduct the interviews and how the interviews will be structured and developed to collect the data. 
 
4.2.1 Semi-structured Interviews  This thesis aims to follow a qualitative research design, through collecting useful data with help from the method that was decided and most suitable for this research. To identify the research question interviews was conducted to get answers that has the greatest feasible quality, which is the initial step in a qualitative study (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.117). Qualitative method does not need the same structure as quantitative method, where a larger amount of data is collected with hypotheses and surveys (Saunders et al., 2016, p.146), and therefore is the qualitative a more natural and logical choice for this study. Another method that often is used among the qualitative sampling is focus groups and group interviews, which focus on recording discussions between participants (Saunders et al., 2016, p.417). Something that could match this thesis well, even though it might be an alternative, interviews could have more length, be more flexible, and if you interview people individually it is less possible to be influenced by others. In this study, with the purpose to understand what could impact the adoption of AI, interviews are better to gain a deeper understanding about the procedure.   When going through different alternatives carefully, semi-structured interviews was chosen because it felt like the most applicable method to collect this study´s data. Benefits of semi-structured interviews is that it enables to focus on the interview among giving the room to ideas and take up new thoughts while going through the interview which can enhance further understanding to the purpose (Adeoye‐Olatunde & Olenik, 2021, p.1360). By conducting individual interviews could different sub-thoughts occur from different people, which could benefit the deepness in the thesis. When organizing a semi-structured interview, it is good to have a guide, but that guide should not be read structured and the same every time, it can be good to vary the questions a bit, depending on which one that is questioned (Adeoye‐Olatunde & Olenik, 2021, p.1362). Allowing the researcher to formulate suitable sub-questions for that specific time and person, assisting the researcher to dig deeper into the process each organization has.   Semi-structured interviews can be managed different ways, such as in-person, by phone or via video (teams), allowing the interviewer to be more present in the questioning and therefore is in-person interviews often preferred since the researcher could capture more than just words (Adeoye‐Olatunde & Olenik, 2021, p.1363). Personal interviews can take longer time than interviews that are through the telephone, but it could also minimize the interaction between the researcher and the respondent. It is good if the interviews have a reasonable timeframe. Therefore, these interviews are expected to be around 45 minutes to give the space the respondents need and give room for sub-questions, and this study will if possible be interviews in person, and if that’s not possible, teams will be preferred.   Even though semi-structured interviews are suited best for this thesis it does not come without any drawbacks, it can be very time-consuming and some problems with controlling the selection of topics and recording the interview and its answers (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.121). The researcher can also have some issues with the collecting of data and analyze them because it could vary from interview to interview, since there could be different aspects of the topics (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.121). When conducting the interviews the timeframe and the structure is an important consideration and the semi-structured interviews suit this research the most given the type of respondents and the nature of the research question.  
 
4.2.2 Interview Guide Semi-structured interviews are quite free and open for different answers and questions. Even if that is a good thing, it can be good to retain some structure to the interviews, therefore a guide has been designed, one for manufacturing companies and one for consultants (see appendices 1-4). The guides have been created to be sure that no important question is missed or that some data could be less relevant for the purpose of the study. The interview guides were created both in English and in Swedish, since the interviews were held in Swedish. The guides are designed to support the research and therefore constructed with the most important questions that could ensure depth in the interview, it could also help the researchers to hold the timeframe. The questions were formulated to be as natural as possible and not risk the participants not fully opening up and telling the researchers their actual thoughts.   In semi-structured interviews, the researchers often have a theme and some key-questions to cover the main purpose, even if they can vary from interview to interview (Saunders et al., 2016, p.390). These interview guides try to focus on different themes and build questions about each theme, they are also created to have simple questions that are easy to understand, therefore some explanations could be done as well. The questions are designed to be open-ended to provide deeper answers and avoid “yes” and “no” responses.   The questions are formed by the theoretical framework and background of this thesis, which is partitioned into different themes that could help in conducting reasonable questions. The themes can be linked to the literature, theories, and common sense and discussions with different approaches (Saunders et al., 2016, p.402). The guides are conducted with an introduction to start with, to let the respondents know about the researchers and describe themselves, like their position in the company and what the organization stands for. 4.3.1 Data Selection  After choosing a research approach, it is important to understand how to select a sample. It is important to consider about the sample and how to use the sampling in the thesis (Saunders et al., 2016, p.272). Saunders et al. (2016, pp.275-276) explain that there are two types of samples that can be selected, there are probability-, and non-probability samples.   Probability sample can be selected from a population where the researcher can select the target that usually is equal for all cases, probability sample is common in quantitative studies and are associated with for example surveys (Saunders et al., 2016, pp.275-276). Non-probability samples can explain as the probability of each case being selected from a target population, the sample selection does not follow a pattern which make the population do not have the equal chance to be chosen, however the researcher may still be able to draw some general conclusions from the selection as well (Saunders et al., 2016, p.276). When using interpretivism as a paradigm it is not needed to analyze the research data statistically with the view to generalize the population from the sample (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.118). Both snowball sampling and purposive sampling is under the interpretivism and non-probability sample, and networking as a sample is also a part of the paradigm (Collis & Hussey, 2021, pp.118-119). While snowball sampling is used where it is crucial to include people with experience of what the thesis is about, and then they mention some that has the same experience of the subject which they can put you in touch with (Collis & Hussey, 2021, pp.118-119). The purposive sampling is quite similar to the snowball sampling because the participants are selected by the researcher and the ones that are chosen has experience of the subject. However, in the purposive sample approach, the researcher chooses the respondents beforehand and do not collect info about other participants from the respondents that are in the study (Collis & Hussey, 2021, p.119).   When selecting the data and samples this thesis has a sort of combination of networking-, snowball- and purposive sampling. 4.4.1 Thematic Analysis  To answer the research question: “What are the barriers and determinants that manufacturing companies in the northern part of Sweden face during the adoption and implementation of AI? “a thematic analysis has been used as an approach. The thematic analysis is an approach that is used to recognize, analyze and present data in different patterns or themes and it is widely used in qualitative studies (Braun & Clarke, 2006, p.79). According to Braun & Clarke (2006, p.77) it is also a very flexible, manageable and highly useful approach to use. The choice of a research method that aligns with the research question is important and a thematic analysis can provide insightful analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006, p.97). The analysis is done in six steps, it starts with familiarizing with the data and ends with producing the report (Braun & Clarke, 2006).   The first step includes transcribing the gathered data and reading through the text repeatedly to fully understand the information and if the data is collected verbally you will need to transcribe the data into written form (Braun & Clarke, 2006, p.87). Since the data collected for this thesis was done through interviews via Teams it was necessary to transcribe everything fully in written text and read through each transcribed interview several times. The transcribing was conducted through the transcribing tool in Microsoft Word. Although the tool transcribed all the interviews, it was necessary to go through the interviews again and the texts, correcting the words and sentences that were incorrect and also capturing body language, enabling further familiarization with the data. However, since both researchers attended the interviews the familiarization process was easier since the researchers had some knowledge before the analysis process and had also been able to collect some initial thoughts and started analyzing the gathered data and identifying themes. The next step is to code the data which means identifying and labeling noteworthy aspects or patterns in the data systematically (Braun & Clarke, 2006, p.88). It was a good start before creating the themes and it also enabled some extra familiarization with the data. Step three follows the initial coding, which means sorting 
 
the different codes into broader potential themes and can be assisted by producing a thematic map that step by step identifies themes that are derived from the codes (Braun & Clarke, 2006, pp.89-90). Eight themes were identified beforehand and were labeled and matched with a color. Then each coded transcriptions were gone through and sorted with the different colors. The process was done twice to ensure that nothing important was missed.   Moving on to step four which involves the refinement of the identified themes, the fourth step includes ensuring that the themes sufficiently capture the collected data, resulting in a better understanding of the themes and offering insight into the collected meaning, patterns, and findings in the data (Braun & Clarke, 2006, pp.91-92). Step five is a continuation of step four and further involves the refinement of the themes with the end goal of clearly defining the themes with names that can reflect their content efficiently (Braun & Clarke, 2006, p.92). Step five was done beforehand but further developed during the process and in this step, eight themes became seven. The last step includes creating a story that communicates the complexity of the analyzed data and provides evidence that there are identified themes that convincingly can give arguments for the research question (Braun & Clarke, 2006, p.93). When the thematic analysis was done, the last step included presenting the empirical findings in a good way and comparing the answers with each other to find differences and similarities.

4.5 Ethical Considerations  Ethical considerations play an important role in business research and researchers must throughout the study re-examine their considerations, however, in recent years there has been significant pressure on researchers regarding ethics, pushing them to display that concerns arising from ethics have been met (Bell & Bryman, 2019, p.112). To address these concerns and create a steady ground for ethical considerations researchers should consider four principles, Avoidance of harm, Informed consent, Privacy and Preventing deception (Bell & Bryman, 2019, p.114; Saunders et al., 2016, p.244).   Avoidance of harm means that the researcher needs to make sure that the participants won’t be harmed in any way due to their participation, which often occurs in situations when participants can’t give their informed consent completely (Bell & Bryman, 2019, p.115). Informed consent on the other hand means that the researchers are required to provide comprehensive information to the participants, ensuring that they fully understand the implications of their involvement and can freely decide whether to participate or not, furthermore that the researchers will honor their agreed terms (Saunders et al., 2016, p.244). Privacy instead means that the participants have the right to their privacy and is very much linked to informed consent and lastly preventing deception, which means preventing the researchers from misleading the participants by presenting false information (Bell & Bryman, 2019, p.124). Overall, the researchers must act ethically and responsibly when collecting data from participants and that the participants understand their rights and what their involvement means as well as their right to withdraw their consent and participation (Saunders et al., 2016, p.244-245).   
 
To conduct the interviews in accordance with Bell & Bryman (2019, p.112-124) and Saunders et al. (2016, p.244-245) principle of ethical considerations, the participants will remain anonymous during the entire process to honor their privacy and avoid any harm that could cause them due to their participation. To honor the principle of informed consent the researchers sent out a document with terms before the interviews, informing the participants about the study and how their personal information will be handled and their right to withdraw their participation at any time. All interviews were recorded and is a term that was disclosed in the document, during the interviews the researchers once again explained the terms and asked the participants if they consented.

6.1 Factors Influencing AI Adoption There are some different factors influencing organizations to adopt AI, it can be driven by technological, organizational, environmental, and behavioral factors (Chattarjee et al., 2021). Something that emerged from the empirical findings is the desire to develop within the organization. There is a common understanding from the participants in the interviews that in order to continue the business, there is a need to develop new technologies and constantly moving forward to prevent falling behind. Sweden is a country that has the most global manufacturing companies per capita in the world (Production2013, n.d. p.165). Therefore, can the desire to develop be crucial for the organizations to stay competitive and continuing the business. However, to ensure development a competitive position in Sweden, future jobs and welfare, Swedish manufacturing companies need to remain innovative and be engaged to develop the industry (Production2023, n.d. p.165). Even though it is important, it is also significant to know how to use AI as a new technology. From the empirical findings, Respondent David revealed the importance of knowing how to use AI to understand how it fits in the company and what tasks that AI can perform and gain most benefits from.   The technology acceptance model (TAM) explains the acceptance for different IT innovations (Hong et al., 2006, p.1822). The model is frequently used to define users´ adoption and the perceived ease of use and usefulness (Hong et al., 2006, p.1822). The empirical findings bring up that adapting new technology as AI can be driven by the need to stay competitive and increasing market shares. Aligning well with the TAM-model and 
 
how it looks at usefulness when adapting new innovation. Perceived usefulness is the individual confidence that a technology can increase and improve performance and be beneficial to the organization by enabling efficiency (Davis, 1989, p.320). Aligning well with the empirical findings and the participants desire to develop AI within their organizations. For example, Respondent C3 says that they understand the need to follow trends and be updated on what comes next with a balance between what to invest in and not.   When it comes to perceived ease of use, it is more about the individual confidence that the technology can be implemented with little effort and be easier to use, therefore it is more likely to be accepted and adopted (Davis, 1989, p.320). Something that occurred in the empirical findings as well, some respondents (both companies and consultants) highlight the benefits with smaller projects when it comes to AI, that is more understandable, and it is easier to adopt a new technology if the organization understands it. However, even though these factors can influence the adoption, the behavioral factors should not be neglected. Some organizations want to feel more comfortable while other want to develop when a new thing arises.    There are some different characteristics when it comes to adoption and adopters. That could both be external requirements (like the sectors that organizations work with), it can also be the cultural and geographical conditions (Damanpour & Schneider, 2006, p.225). While it also can be managerial factors that can affect the adoption of innovation within organizations, some variables like age, gender, and education did not have a significant effect on adoption, it is more about how the managers attitudes are and how they lead their employees (Damanpour & Schneider, 2006, p.228-229). One perspective that emerged from the interviews was leadership and its importance. The empirical findings highlight how good leadership can be defined. It can be defined as a leader that is updated on the “trends” and new technologies, most of the participants indicate that being informed and updated about AI is a key to adopt AI. The managers attitudes can affect the whole adoption, that is something that the respondents also highlight. Respondent C3 argues that a good leader is positive and that makes it easier to introduce new innovations like AI without resistance. The empirical findings also mention the magnitude of being a leader that present a change in a nice way to meet the employees need and thoughts.   Different characteristics among leaders can make it easier or harder to adopt AI. From the empirical findings it was highlighted that the characteristics of leadership in the manufacturing industry can be a problem with its traditional waterfall-system when it comes to adoption. Respondent Erik argues that the leader plays an even bigger role in the manufacturing industries, they must have a more agile approach when implementing AI.   
6.1.1 The Internal Perspective The internal perspective of AI adoption is important to understand to grasp the intentions and the behavioral factors to adopt technology. The TAM model understands the behavioral factors and explains that it doesn’t matter how good the technology is if the user doesn’t perceive the technology as useful and easy to adapt (Bryan & Zuva, 2021, p.139). From the empirical findings, a lot of the respondents viewed AI as crucial to adopt and mentioned that it is an important part of staying competitive. However, Respondent C3 explains that there is internal resistance, something that Respondent David argues may 
 
occur in the manufacturing industry. Respondent C2 also mentioned that the manufacturing industry is very traditional and slow toward changes, C2 further says that the leaders lack awareness of the technology and view it as too complex. It is interesting because internal resistance may cause barriers to the adoption process, not convincing the perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use within the entire organization. However, Respondent C2 believes that the internal view of AI might change after implementation.   However, the TAM model can’t capture the full measure of behavior since it involves factors like personality, individual traits and social values (Malatji et al., 2020, p.115). In the empirical findings, Respondent C5 explained what is affecting the different attitudes towards AI are for example age and personality. According to C5, some may be more comfortable with their situation, and it could be because there is an integrated fear based on their inexperience with AI.   Implementing new innovations is done in three steps, where the first step is initiation, which includes becoming aware of the innovation, developing attitudes, and initial evaluation (Frambach, & Schillewaert, 2002, p.164). The attitudes are important to address and as mentioned, in the empirical findings the attitude towards AI is that it is interesting although they might face some internal resistance, Respondent C4 also explained that they might not fully understand what AI will entail. According to Damanpour, & Schneider (2006, p.229) what is important is the managerial characteristics, if the top management has good attitudes towards entrepreneurship and competition it can have a positive impact on the persuasion of innovation adoption. Hence, the attitudes within the entire organization, particularly those within top management, significantly influence the adoption process. The viewpoint and determinants from the management and employees are magnified as crucial in technology adoption and can be explained with the TOE framework (Chattarjee et al., 2021, p.102403). Once again referring back to the internal attitudes of some of the managers within company C2 and C3, although, Respondent C3 mentioned that they tried to educate the top management to overcome the resistance. According to Na et al. (2022) is also significant in the adoption of AI, the culture, support, and involvement within the entire organization. Furthermore, the acceptance and how the companies integrate innovation within the organization serves a role (Frambach, & Schillewaert, 2002, p.164). What was found in the empirical findings was that companies should try AI in smaller projects, identifying the potential and effects of the adoption. Moreover, they should in an educational way explain and update the employees about what is going on, creating a general understanding of AI which generates better communication and acceptance within the organization.   Rogers innovation diffusion is a theory that intends to describe the rate of adoption when it comes to innovation (Rogers, 2002, p.221). There are five attributes that explain the rate of adoption where complexity is one of these attributes. Complexity simply means how difficult an innovation is to use and understand (Rogers, 2002, p.257). According to Respondent C2 who claimed that the top management had neither looked at nor discussed AI and how it could be adopted in their organization, AI could be considered too complex for them to understand. Connecting the findings with Rogers innovation diffusion theory, it becomes clear that there are important attributes such as complexity that affect the rate of adoption.   
 
Another attribute that affects the rate of adoption is trialability which means that innovations that are tested in controlled settings are more likely to be adopted than those who are not tested (Rogers, 2002, p.258). The need for early adopters is emphasized by Respondent Erik who argued that they can be the driving force towards change structure. However, while some of the respondents claim that they might be open to being early adopters a lot of them would also like to see proof that AI actually works first, which is contradictive and interesting to explore. What would be interesting to understand is whether they would rather like to see AI implemented at another manufacturing company for example a competitor or by other external actors such as AI providers. The only one who states that they don’t prefer being early adopters but rather “copycats” is Respondent C2. Moreover, the majority of the companies and also the consultants state that it is important to implement AI in order to stay competitive and keep or increase their respective market shares. Respondent C1 further highlights that they want to stay ahead of their competitors. But how can companies stay ahead if they want to see that AI is tested elsewhere beforehand? Consequently, stressing that Rogers theory can be applied to understand the rate of adoption. Further, Uzumcu & Acilmis (2023) describe late adopters as more suspicious about innovations while early adopters are viewed as role models. Indicating that some of the respondents might be a bit suspicious which can also be proven in the empirical findings.    AI technology will change the business environment both in short-term and long-term, calling for strategies and plans on how to address short-term goals as well as long-term goals (Ghobakhloo 2018, p.910-926). While the respondents have started to grasp what AI might entail for them, there are still a lot of uncertainties. Respondent C5 explained that they focus on how they can automate their processes in general and that AI can be a part of the automation but not exclusively, which indicates that they don’t have a clear plan for AI, similar to the rest of the respondents. Additionally, respondent C2 also lack a clear plan and clarifies that they have made significant improvements such as digitalization but with no plan on how to utilize the data.   
6.1.2 Opportunities & Barriers Looking into the adoption of AI it is good to know about both opportunities and barriers that can emerge during the process and that the organization could face while implementing AI. When debating what an opportunity with AI is it is important to check if it is a relative advantage, which is a measure of the innovation being better than the existing solution it replaces (Rogers, 2002, p.229). It is often expressed in economic profitability and social prestige and it is important to see the relative advantages to later have a better chance to adopt it (Rogers, 2002, p.229). The empirical findings highlight that an AI solution could be a relative advantage and that AI could be even better in the future. It also mentions the magnitude of doing the daily work better than it is now. Most of the participants sees the relative advantages in the administrative tasks, that may be because it is easier to implement AI there, without a huge project that involves their core business and the manufacturing.   The empirical findings show a lot of opportunities with AI adoption in the manufacturing industry, it could be forecasting and planning or the possibility of helping in decision-making to make better and faster decisions without human errors, which could do the whole organizations more qualitative. The culture in the organization and how well they can adopt it can be both a driver and a barrier according to Ghobakhloo (2022, p.1044). 
 
In the empirical findings it can be argued that most of the companies that were interviewed was hopeful and saw AI as a thing that could change and make the production better, which indicates that the managers are a driver to adopt. While it also was some negative thoughts about AI, or fear of what is going to happened with the business which could be a barrier to implementing AI.   AI can acquire several benefits, like cutting costs and reducing errors. With AI, organizations can decrease on employees or assign them other jobs (Ahmed et al., 2022, p.5038). Something that emerged in the empirical findings, is that most of the managers that were interviewed pointed out how people that may be replaced could do more value-creating activities. Which was discussed with the consultant David if that is something they just say because they have to or if they actually mean it. That is something that is left for us to see in the future. It was also discussed how to find new employees in the northern part of Sweden, AI may not replace people, employees do not exist in the amount that are needed.   Even though there are a lot of opportunities with AI Respondent Erik highlight the importance of doing small processes first, assisting the organizations to grasp it more easily and then after gaining understandings, find where AI could have the best use, even if it is in the production or in the administrative tasks.   There are opportunities, but opportunities do not come without barriers. One barrier that could emerge is that the technology is to complex and require a lot of data (Ghobakhloo, 2022, p.1039). The two external consultants from the empirical findings agrees that AI may be too complex, because AI is dependent on big amount of data and the organizations may not know how to use the big amount of data in the right, or most useful way. It could be that they do not have big data within the company or that they do not know what to do with it. Which can also cause that the timeframe of adopting AI is longer than it could. The lengthy process of implementing AI was highlighted as a barrier by most of the respondents.     Furthermore, due to the empirical findings a huge barrier to adopt AI is the company secrets and how to keep them within the company if you have an AI-solution. For the manufacturing industry, company secrets are important, and it could be hard for them to stay competitive if their secrets become public. The barrier did not occur in the theoretical literature but is still an important aspect to take into consideration for organizations. It is important to use safe AI-solutions within the company, and for that the organizations need the right competence.   Which leads to another big barrier that occur in both the empirical findings and the literature from the theory, that is the lack of competence about AI. The adoption of AI and the process of being more automated can decrease the need of human capacity and therefore avoid high labor costs, however it creates a paradox (Edh Mirzaei et al., 2021, p.281). When using AI and more automated processes the need for knowledge-based manufacturing will arise and people with high competence can be hard to find in a high-cost environments like Sweden (Edh Mirzaei et al., 2021, p.281). Competence appeared to be one of the biggest barriers or challenges in the empirical findings, there is a lack of competence both internally and externally, especially in the northern part of Sweden. To acquire a skilled workforce, investments in education, training and technological innovations will be necessary (Edh Mirzaei et al., 2021, p.281). 
 
 All the participants argued that competence was one of the biggest barriers to adopt AI, but even with competence it could be hard to adopt AI because of the high costs that comes with it. One of the main identified barriers in Sweden is the cost of adopting AI (Tillväxtanalys, 2021, p.20). The high costs could affect how organizations do with the implementation of AI. If they know it’s going to be a long timeframe when adopting it, they can be reluctant to adopting AI.   Although it can be expensive to implement AI, especially in the short run. One of the respondents in the empirical findings David argues that AI could be more cost-efficient in the long run and some other of the participants don’t see another choice than implementing it if they want to stay competitive and not falling behind.  
 
6.2 The Role of Business Networks The role of business networks is both highlighted in the theoretical framework as well as the empirical findings. Mazzola et al. (2009, p.387) argue that it is crucial and necessary to enable efficiency, expand globally, gather knowledge, and take advantage of technological advancements, making it possible to stay competitive. Moreover, looking at the TOE framework the environment plays an important role in innovation adoption and explains the business arena with the industry, competitors, and government interactions (Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990, cited in, Zhu et al., 2006, pp.1559-1560). The empirical findings display that the network does matter and Respondent C4 mentions that they are in contact with a partner to discuss AI further. Moreover, the ERP system is also mentioned as a part of the process. However, while Respondent C3 mentioned that it would be beneficial to collaborate with the network and connect suppliers, C3 also argues that it could endanger their company secrets and competitive advantages. However, to ensure lean manufacturing which is the goal for a lot of manufacturing companies, and especially JIT, collaboration and relationships with subcontractors and other inter-firm relationships are important and allows better Industry 4.0 implementation (Vargas et al., 2024, p.95). It is interesting since Respondent David argued that AI specifically could enable better JIT.   What is interesting is that in the theoretical framework, a lot of the literature magnified the importance of business networks in innovation adoption while there was little evidence of the importance of business networks in the empirical findings. Instead, the respondents highlighted that it is good with business networks but also risky. However, Respondent Erik highlighted that the companies should value the relationship and network with the universities to access the younger generation with the right competence. Putting pressure on the universities to be more aligned with AI technology.    Another thing that aligned the theoretical framework and the empirical findings was customer demand. According to Production2030 (n.d) the demand from consumers has shifted to require more customized products which implies that companies must enable flexible productions which can be achieved through automation and digitalization. In the empirical findings, many of the respondents highlighted that they would see the demand for AI solutions from customers first.   
 
6.2.1 External competence  According to the empirical findings, the biggest contribution to AI adoption was external competence. All the respondents agreed that in order to carry out AI implementation, external actors must contribute with project management, tools and competence. Tillväxtanalays (2021, p.18) argues that interfirm relationships are important and valuable especially when companies lack the in-house competence and capabilities to carry out the adoption. However, Respondent David mentioned that in the northern part of Sweden, there is a general lack of sufficient AI competence that can assist the companies. Meanwhile, external competence is needed together with internal competence, to lead and push AI adoption, Respondent C5 highlights that external actors should provide a toolbox with various tools for companies to choose from. Tools that are not standardized and can be adjusted to the specific business. Furthermore, according to Respondent C1, if AI exists in a SaaS solution the implementation will come by default. Which is interesting since that would indicate that the companies could access external competence and AI without the competence working directly with the firm, overcoming the obstacle of less competence accessible in the northern part of Sweden. Moreover, what also is interesting is that AI exists in a SaaS solution that many of these companies already use Microsoft Office, but it has not yet been exploited. Maybe because it is not operating in Swedish yet, but what is curious is that none of the respondents except for David mentioned that Microsoft’s AI solution existed.  
6.2.2 Government Incentives & Regulatory Framework Thoughts about government incentives and regulatory framework’s roles in AI adoption were interesting to explore and appeared to be something hard to determine when going through the empirical findings. While the majority of the respondents argued that regulatory frameworks are necessary to make companies feel safe when implementing AI, respondents C3 and C4 argued the opposite. Respondent C5 highlights that regulations are needed since AI eventually will become smarter than us. However, Respondent Erik and C5 said that although the regulatory framework is important, they might not be able to rely on it since regulations are often behind.   Although Respondent C3 brought up that regulatory frameworks are not important in AI adoption, C3 mentioned that regulation may be needed in the case of patent-related questions. Something that only C3 brought up and could be something interesting to further explore. How can companies keep their competitive advantages when AI becomes more and more adopted in research and development?   What also divided the respondents was the need for government incentives. During the past years, the Swedish government has made several attempts to push the adoption of AI technology. With the first attempt to achieve a more competitive manufacturing industry through pushing the adoption of Industry 4.0 practices (Regeringen, 2018). The second attempt was in December 2023 intending to strengthen and develop AI in Sweden in general (Regeringen, 2023). While Respondent C1 believes that government incentives in general are important and exemplifies it with the incentives to drive an electric vehicle, Respondent C3 disagrees. C3 argues that government incentives such as financial aid will rather cause dishonest competition and points out that the companies should instead be curious by nature. Which is an interesting observation because what should be successful drivers of AI adoption, the push from external actors such as governments or curiosity and desire to develop within the companies?  
 
6.3 The Impact of AI Adoption When studying innovation one of the key factors for the business to succeed with an adoption relates to a decision how to use the innovation in the organization (Frambach & Schillewaert, 2002, p.163). The empirical findings show that all the respondents agreed that there are numerous benefits with implementing AI, and how that could increase their performance and their intention to stay competitive.   Respondent C2 mention that AI could make the business grow without hiring more people, which makes them more cost-efficient. Sharma et al. (2023, p.390) mention how AI can enhance productivity by reducing human involvement in tasks where AI is generally better and can do more qualitative products. It can be hard to dismiss employees to make room for AI, but the empirical findings also show how the employees might not exist in the northern part of Sweden, and therefore organizations can grow faster without being dependent on people that may not exist. The adoption of AI can also create cultural changes within the organization, and that can lead to a more flexible workflow (Sharma et al., 2023, p.390). Aligning with the answers from the empirical findings about how to make employees do more value-creating things for the organization. However, as mentioned earlier, Respondent David sees it as an excuse for hiding the true intentions, that is laying off employees.   From the theoretical literature it could be found that manufacturing companies have to have an understanding about the adoption of the process because of the constant changes and improvements (Sharma et al., 2023, p.390). The understandings are important to continue developing the organizations, and to stay competitive. Although a lot of the respondents sees the potential with AI and how that could increase the performance, some participants mentions that they may not see the benefits right when they adopt AI, that will come in the long run. Because of that, the dare to invest is a key, even if the results may not come right away, they most give the opportunity and work with AI and then after a while see the results of it. The general impacts of AI will increase in the long run, although it seems important to adopt AI in the nearest future to stay competitive.   Kinkel et al. (2022, p.1) mention that it has become more important for manufacturing organizations to adopt AI technologies because of the potential to increase sales and optimizing processes. That is something the participants mention in the empirical findings as well. They all agreed that an adoption of AI will give them a competitive advantage and it is crucial to implement to stay competitive. Sweden´s overall competitiveness is dependent on the condition arising from research and competence that exist in the country. Even if the respondents are positive to implementing AI. The adoption of AI in production and logistics is still relatively low (Kinkel et al., 2022, p.3).   
6.3.1 Performance The empirical findings also show that the respondents agree on the potential to increase their financial performance, with margins that increases and derives from the more efficient processes and decreased costs. Wacker (1996, p.484) explains that the manufacturing companies that manage to do it most efficiently while those that enabling the best quality are the ones that can be most competitive. AI could solve both efficiency and being more competitive. Respondent C1 highlight the huge potential to increase margins and financial performance and remain a global actor and it is why adopting AI in the future is crucial. Respondent C4 agree and sees AI as a potential to make processes 
 
more effective and have better control of the quality and products. Aligning with the literature that sate how new technologies could build new business models and make new and better processes that can give higher revenues among with greater competitiveness and higher efficiency.   
6.3.2 Timeframe for Adoption The manufacturing industry is characterized as being cost-intensive and can benefit from AI adoption, still, only 4 percent of the Swedish manufacturing companies have adopted AI, it can be compared with the ICT (Information, communication, and technology) sector where 21 percent has adopted AI in their organizations (Tillväxtanalys. 2020, p.14). That is something that is proven in the empirical findings as well. Neither of the organizations has implemented AI fully in their business, the participants agree that an AI implementation is a few years away, even though some of the respondents think it’s just one year away, while others say that it could be up to five years before implementation. However, Respondent David said that companies could utilize AI solutions even sooner, particularly in administrative tasks. Since Microsoft Office 356 already provides an AI solution but not in Swedish at the moment, but shortly. Interestingly, the organizations that are most educated and interested in AI are the ones that believe AI adoption will be implemented in the near future.

7. Conclusion   
This chapter provides a conclusion with the main objective to answer the research question. Furthermore, the chapter will include the study’s contribution to literature, followed by managerial and societal implications. Moreover, the limitations and advice for future research will be presented.   
7.1 General Conclusion The aim of this qualitative study was to explore the barriers and determinants manufacturing companies in the northern part of Sweden face when adopting AI. The chosen research methodology was an inductive approach that guided the entire thesis from the reviewed literature and semi-structured interviews with managers from manufacturing companies and external consultants. The findings from the conducted interviews and the literature presented in the theoretical framework provided a base for the analysis and discussion to answer the research question:    “What are the barriers and determinants that manufacturing companies in the northern 
part of Sweden face during the adoption and implementation of AI? “  Previous research showed that there is a lack of comprehensive understanding regarding what barriers and determinants Swedish manufacturing companies face when considering AI adoption. Manufacturing companies are behind in the adoption of AI, even though these organizations are the ones that might benefit the most from AI implementation (McKinsey, 2023). Moreover, previous research showed that there is a need for further understanding of the different drivers and barriers in AI adoption (Tillväxtanalys, 2021, p.24). This thesis used the TOE and TAM combined to understand the adoption of AI as suggested by Chatterjee et al. (2021, p.11). Further, together with other important concepts and theories a conceptual framework was created. The empirical findings supported the initial theories and concepts and also added important factors to the conceptual framework (see figure 7), resulting in a good understanding of AI adoption.   By comparing the theoretical framework with the empirical findings some main themes that could help explain AI adoption were identified. While most themes were recognized in the literature, leadership emerged during the interviews as well as an enhanced focus on external competence. Resulting in a broader understanding of the organizations’ view of AI and their intentions to adopt and implement the technology. The conceptual framework (figure 7) is a good map to use to understand the adoption and implementation process. The list below contains the factors that have the greatest impact on AI adoption according to the empirical findings, with support from the literature:   
 Leadership was mentioned as an important factor, in influencing and guiding employees. Good leadership means, good communication along with educated and informed leaders with a positive attitude.   
 External competence was something that every respondent highlighted as crucial when adopting AI. The demand for competence is high and will continue to increase and there is a significant lack of AI competence in the northern part of Sweden. According to the managers that were interviewed, external competence is key to carry out an implementation.  
 
  Attitudes and understanding were obvious factors influencing AI adoption. The 
respondents who were more knowledgeable and felt optimistic about AI were also the ones who would implement AI within the shortest timeframe. When managers and employees are well-informed and positive, the adoption becomes easier, highlighting the need for education and communication to create a good AI environment.   
 Opportunities and barriers were other valuable factors when thinking about adopting AI in a business. The literature identified these factors as a gap that has to be filled to fully understand AI adoption. The empirical findings revealed a lot of opportunities and barriers, for example, how AI could help the organization with decision-making is an opportunity while the lack of understanding of AI was seen as one of the biggest barriers. However, another big barrier was the cost of implementing AI.  
 In conclusion, this thesis aims to explore the barriers and determinants manufacturing companies in the northern part of Sweden face when adopting AI, and which factors that could drive or hold back an implementation. The study also identified how the adoption process is related to the TAM and TOE models and how it had many similarities with the factors that were identified in the empirical findings. It was also the case with other concepts and models. The theories with help from the conducted interviews formed a conceptual framework that could explain what barriers and determinants manufacturing companies in the northern part of Sweden face. Therefore, this thesis provides knowledge about the barriers and determinants, answering the research question. It is obvious that AI is here to stay and that disruptive changes will occur in different sectors globally. As revealed in the empirical findings, companies are left with no choice, those resistant to AI may face extinction. Putting pressure on companies to continue to develop as Henry Ford (1923) said, “Businesses that grow by development and improvement do not die.”.   
7.2 Contributions to Literature As explained, this thesis aims to contribute with knowledge concerning the factors affecting AI adoption in manufacturing companies in the northern part of Sweden, particularly the barriers and determinants they face. The researchers, therefore, used the combined TOE-TAM model as Chattarjee et al. (2021, p.11) proposed to future researchers, to explain AI adoption, moreover, the researchers combined it with the attributes from Rogers’s innovation diffusion theory, business network and leadership (see figure 7). The study could together with these factors explain what affects AI adoption.    Furthermore, the thesis provides insight into what opportunities, determinants and barriers the different companies and external consultants see from AI adoption, which is a contribution to the gap that Tillväxtanalys (2021, p.24) acknowledged with the need for a conception of the drivers and barriers of AI adoption. Further, Tillväxtanalys (2021, p.21) assumed that manufacturing companies experienced cost as a barrier and also that they require a high level of precision, however, it was not empirically explored. This thesis can contribute to the assumption and provide proof that companies experience cost as a barrier. Moreover, the requirement for a high level of precision was also an assumption that this thesis could prove right. The empirical findings showed that the 
 
companies would like to see that AI work before implementation and that they are concerned about their company secrets. They also highlight that they will begin implementing AI on small scales and in administrative tasks, which can reveal that they are afraid that AI might not be as precise and secure as they might hope for to utilize it in more important tasks.   One of the biggest barriers to AI adoption was that companies rely on external competence in order to enable the adoption. This thesis has contributed with information and also discovered that there is a significant lack of sufficient competence about AI and particularly in the northern part of Sweden. Additionally, the thesis provided insight into the different timeframes before we can see AI adopted in the manufacturing industry. It is valuable information since the Swedish government has pushed the manufacturing industry to adopt Industry 4.0 where AI is included since 2018 (Regeringen, 2018). The contribution is that although AI has been around for several years, it is fairly new to the manufacturing industry and the empirical findings shows that AI adoption is a few years away.   Lastly, it is important to state that this study is not aiming to create a complete theory and understanding of AI adoption in the manufacturing industry in the northern part of Sweden, but rather to contribute with good understanding of factors affecting the adoption.   
7.3 Managerial Implications This thesis empirical findings are built on interviews with managers from the manufacturing industry and external consultants who work towards AI and an implementation of it. The answers that were conducted gave several insights into what is important for the process of adopting AI. The most vital factors that drive the process are conducted into a conceptual framework (see figure 7). One of the insights that were highlighted in the empirical findings was how important leadership was. Leadership includes having good communication with the employees on what is going on and how the process is going to take place.   It is also important to look at the internal perspective and attitudes, to succeed with an AI adoption. With that said, increase the understanding among the employees is a crucial thing to do if the organization want the adoption process to go smooth. Being a successful leader could also imply that managers should set an example, like being proactive and positive towards innovations overall. Which could have a huge impact on the employees. It can be seen from the empirical findings that if a manager is positive and have an understanding about AI, it is more likely that the whole organization have a good attitude towards it and moving faster towards an implementation.   However, even if leadership is a crucial factor for the organizations that adopt AI, it is also central that the companies start with smaller projects as the larger changes demand more from the organization, in both understanding and costs. To start and develop AI and its understanding during the time of the small implementation allows the company to learn more about AI in practice without doing a big and expensive implementation right away. It is important to see where AI can be the first benefits and not the largest. AI may have the first benefits in administrative tasks, where it is easier to adopt. It can be an advantage for the organization to implement AI there first to gain understanding and be 
 
more competent before implementing AI in the actual production. When doing smaller AI projects first and when the knowledge is better, the company is at the forefront and therefore may stand out and stay or gain competitiveness.  Interestingly, accessible AI-solutions exist which could be a start to do smaller AI projects. For example, Microsoft Office 365 provides a simple AI that companies can utilize, although it is only in English at the moment. Managers could look at accessible providers such as Microsoft to enable easy and smaller AI usages to take the first steps towards a bigger implementation.   The empirical findings revealed how important it is to be informed and well-read about AI and the development of AI. It is significant for organizations, even if they are not planning to adopt AI in the nearest future. However, the answers from the interviews agreed that AI is something that needs an adoption sooner or later to stay competitive, and therefore it is important to have the knowledge so they can evaluate it and see where the largest benefits are.  
7.4 Societal Implications Considering the societal implications of this thesis, the emerging topic of AI has awoken a lot of concerns about what AI might entail for society and pressures companies and governments to address the emergent AI topic in a safe and ethical way. One of the major concerns is that AI will replace a lot of jobs, causing major layoffs. Replacing employees was highlighted by the respondents and it was quite clear that an eventual AI implementation will not cause layoff but would instead allow the employees to do more value creating activities, even though such statements might be a cover up to hide the true intentions of the companies.   However, in the northern part of Sweden there is a lack of significant competence and an overall labor shortage. Therefore, AI can be seen as a contribution to the workforce and not a replacement. Nevertheless, AI can replace the human factor in different tasks, but companies is still in need of the human involvement. What was found in the empirical findings is that AI may allow companies to grow without recruiting more employees. Allowing companies to continue to exist in the northern part of Sweden and satisfy the need for more competence. Furthermore, the need for ethical guidelines especially in labor-related questions should be developed.  Therefore, an implication is that the government should develop laws and regulations that can assist and guide companies in the adoption of AI. The government could also create different incentives to push AI implementation. However, some respondents did not believe in government incentives and argued that companies should be the one driving the adoption, to get a more impartial competition. Further, other regulatory frameworks that may be important to develop are patent-related questions and where to draw the line when it comes to AI generated innovations.   As stated by Regeringen (2018), AI can make Sweden more competitive on the global arena and is therefore important to implement. AI adoption in general will go fast and for companies to not lose market shares and stay competitive, the society in general must accept and push the adoption.   
 
7.5 Limitations  This thesis does not come without any limitations. One of the biggest limitations of this study is the timeframe that was approximately four months. It did not allow to fully dig deep and gain all the knowledge about every component in the theoretical models and concepts. Some key-concept of models has therefore potentially been left out or not entirely been brought up. Due to the limitation, the thesis has relied on existing theories and models but has developed and combined these with other factors, theories, and models to provide a more comprehensive framework.  Since the timeframe was quite short, another limitation became the ability to get a larger number of respondents that contribute to the study. This study had seven interviews, five representatives from different manufacturing companies and two external consultants. A larger number of companies were asked to participate but some didn’t answer or simply said no to an interview. Even if this thesis aimed for more respondents to get a broader understanding of AI adoption, the interviews that was conducted gave a good understanding about what barriers and determinants manufacturing companies experience when adopting AI. Although, when seven interviews were done it was possible to see patterns in the answers.   A third limitation was that the answers from the interview were translated from Swedish to English. Something that one should have in mind when reading the empirical findings and analysis together with the discussion. The quotes and answers are translated in the best possible way, although it is important to acknowledge. Another limitation of this study is that none of the organizations that were interviewed had implemented AI fully. Which has led to guesses from the respondents regarding what impact AI can have on the manufacturing industry, the outlined performance and outcomes that have been emphasized in the empirical findings are guesses from the respondents.   
7.6 Future Research Suggestions Since this thesis is limited to manufacturing companies in the northern part of Sweden, it could be interesting to expand and look at Sweden as a whole. It may allow researchers to compare the different situations that may occur due to geographical differences. It could be interesting to understand if the need for external competence and the lack of external competence are the same in other parts of Sweden. Moreover, since leadership emerged as a big topic during the interviews, future research could dig deeper into the topic and how it affects AI adoption. For example, look at how the attitudes, positive or negative of leaders influence the adoption process.   This study included relatively large companies in terms of revenue and employees, it would therefore be interesting for future research to look at smaller organizations, like SMEs (Small and Medium-sized Enterprises) and see how they handle these kinds of processes. It would also be interesting to investigate how different organizational structures handle AI adoption, for instance, hierarchical vs flat organizations.   Lastly, this thesis was not able to fully see the actual performance and outcome of an AI adoption due to the fact that none of the companies had implemented AI fully. Future research should utilize the possibility of investigating companies that have implemented AI and look at financial and organizational performance.
