# Source use policy

The repository separates **content value for thesis writing** from **citation quality**.

## Core rule

A source must not be rejected only because it is not a peer-reviewed paper or because it is inconvenient to cite.

A video, seminar, lecture, interview, technical talk, documentation page, expert presentation, transcript, thesis, repository, workshop page, or other non-paper source may still be important **theory material** if it contains relevant and credible information that should influence the thesis.

The primary question is:

> Does this source contain scientifically or technically useful material that should influence what we write in the thesis?

Citation suitability is a separate question.

## Two independent dimensions

### 1. Thesis-content value

Classify the source by how useful its content is for understanding, synthesis, methodology, interpretation, or theory:

- `core-theory`: directly important to the thesis argument or conceptual framework.
- `supporting-theory`: useful for explanation, interpretation, design choices, examples, or additional context.
- `implementation-context`: useful mainly for implementation or operational understanding.
- `discovery-only`: useful only for locating stronger or more complete material.
- `irrelevant`: no meaningful contribution to the thesis.

### 2. Citation grade

Independently record whether the source is suitable for formal citation-grade evidence:

- `primary-strong`: primary scientific source with stable, checkable claims.
- `secondary-strong`: credible secondary source suitable for some claims.
- `informal-expert`: credible expert material such as a seminar, lecture, interview, or technical presentation.
- `unstable-transcript`: relevant content is available but transcription, timestamps, equations, or wording are not sufficiently stable for precise citation.
- `not-citation-grade`: useful material, but not appropriate as formal evidence for a precise claim.

A low citation grade **does not imply rejection from thesis-content use**.

## Expert videos and talks

For a relevant expert video or seminar:

1. Preserve the original language.
2. Identify the speaker, affiliation, expertise, event, and date when reliably available.
3. Extract the concepts, distinctions, mechanisms, examples, assumptions, caveats, and methodological advice that matter to the thesis.
4. Record timestamps when available; otherwise record the semantic section of the talk.
5. Distinguish the speaker's explanation or interpretation from a formally verified theorem or experimental result.
6. If an exact theorem, numerical result, or strong empirical claim is important, locate the underlying primary paper when possible.
7. Do **not** discard a valuable explanation merely because a primary paper exists elsewhere.

## Writing policy

Theory synthesis may use information learned from all credible theory-material sources, not only citation-grade sources.

When a non-citation-grade source contributes an important idea:

- keep the idea in the theory-material layer;
- preserve its provenance;
- corroborate exact scientific claims with primary literature when practical;
- avoid overstating uncertain details caused by noisy transcription or incomplete context.

The final thesis prose should be built from the **best available understanding of the material**, rather than from a citation-count optimization process.

## Rejection policy

Reject a source from thesis-content use only when at least one of the following applies:

- it is scientifically or technically irrelevant to the thesis;
- it is materially unreliable, misleading, or unsupported;
- the available content is too corrupted or incomplete to recover useful meaning;
- it is an exact duplicate and adds no independent content;
- it is superseded by another stored version and adds no unique material.

Do not reject solely because the format is YouTube, transcript, GitHub, lecture, workshop, blog, documentation, or another non-paper medium.

## Language rule

Raw sources, extracted theory material, evidence, and source-derived notes remain in the source's original language. Translation belongs only to a later, explicit writing/translation stage and is not part of the canonical source corpus.
